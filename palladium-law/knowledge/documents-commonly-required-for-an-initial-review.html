Documents Commonly Required for an Initial Review

A good initial review is built on the record, not the recollection. Here is what to gather, why each item matters, and what to do when something is missing.

When we review a matter for the first time, the quality of our assessment is directly proportional to the completeness of the file. You do not need to organise anything perfectly — assembling and ordering the record is part of our work — but knowing what to look for will save days, and occasionally it will change the assessment itself.

1. The agreement

Whatever formed the basis of the arrangement: a signed contract, terms and conditions you accepted online, an engagement letter, an exchange of emails that amounted to an agreement, or the terms published on a website at the time you relied on them.

  • If you accepted terms online, note the date — terms change, and the version that binds you is the one in force when you agreed.
  • Screenshots of a website as it appeared at the relevant time can be significant evidence. If you have them, keep them; never delete an account that still holds them.

2. Proof of payment

Bank statements, card statements, transfer confirmations, receipts or invoices covering every payment connected to the matter. These establish the two facts every financial dispute begins with: how much left your account, and where it went.

  • Statements should show the full transaction line — date, amount, reference and beneficiary — not a cropped fragment.
  • If payments went through an intermediary (a payment processor, a platform, an exchange), include the intermediary's records too.

3. The correspondence

Emails, letters and messages between you and the other party — including the messages that feel unflattering to your own position. Correspondence usually contains the admissions, promises and contradictions on which a matter turns.

  • Export message threads whole, rather than forwarding selected extracts; context is evidence.
  • Messaging apps matter as much as email. Screenshots are acceptable; native exports are better.
  • If contact happened by telephone, write a short note of each call from memory: date, who spoke, what was said.

4. Identity of the other party

Anything that identifies who you were actually dealing with: company names and numbers, website addresses, the names used by individuals, account details payments were sent to. Counterparties in disputes are not always who they appeared to be, and establishing the correct legal identity early determines who any claim is properly addressed to.

5. Prior complaints and responses

If you have already complained — to the counterparty, to your bank, or to any other body — include the complaint and every response, especially any letter described as a “final response.” These documents define what has already been decided, what deadlines may already be running, and which routes remain open.

If something is missing

Do not let a gap in the file delay your enquiry. Records can often be recovered: banks can reissue statements, email providers retain archives, and companies can be required to provide copies of what they hold about you. Tell us what existed and we will advise on what can be retrieved — and on how much the gap actually matters.

Send the record as it is, not as you wish it were. An honest, incomplete file is far more useful than a polished, partial one.

A note on originals

Never send us your only copy of anything. Work from copies, keep originals safe, and preserve devices and accounts that hold relevant material until the matter is fully concluded.
