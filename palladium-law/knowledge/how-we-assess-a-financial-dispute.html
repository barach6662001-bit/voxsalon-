How We Assess a Financial Dispute

Before any letter is written or any claim is raised, a dispute has to be understood. This is how we do it — and how we decide whether a matter is worth pursuing at all.

Most people arrive at a financial dispute mid-story. Money has been paid, something has gone wrong, correspondence has soured, and by the time professional advice is sought the situation feels tangled beyond repair. The first thing we do is untangle it — and the discipline we apply is the same in every matter, whether the sum involved is modest or substantial.

First: the story, in your own words

The initial discussion is deliberately informal. We ask you to tell us what happened as you experienced it: what you were promised, what you paid, what you received, and when things began to deviate from the agreement. At this stage we are not testing your account — we are mapping it. Legal categories come later; the sequence of events comes first.

Two questions matter more than any others in this conversation: what exactly were you entitled to expect? and what evidence exists of that expectation? Almost everything that follows depends on the answers.

Second: the documents

A dispute is only as strong as its record. We ask for the contract or terms you agreed to, proof of the payments made, and the correspondence between you and the other party. We read all of it — not a summary of it — because experience shows the decisive detail is usually buried in an attachment nobody thought was important.

From the documents we construct a chronology: every date, every payment, every promise and every deviation, in order. A chronology does two things. It reveals the true shape of the dispute, and it exposes the gaps — the missing invoice, the undocumented phone call — while there is still time to address them.

Third: the honest questions

Once the record is assembled, we test the matter against the questions that actually decide it:

  • Is there a recognisable basis for the claim? A disappointing outcome is not automatically an actionable one. We look for a breached term, a misrepresentation, an unauthorised transaction — something with legal substance.
  • What does the evidence prove, rather than suggest? We assess the file the way the other side, or a neutral third party, would read it.
  • Who is the claim actually against — and are they worth pursuing? A strong claim against a counterparty who has disappeared or holds no assets may be worth very little in practice.
  • What would pursuing it cost, in money and in time? Proportionality is a legal principle, but it is also common sense. The remedy should be worth the road to it.
  • What are the realistic routes? Negotiation, a formal complaint, pre-action correspondence, escalation to a formal process — each has different prospects, costs and timescales in each case.
The most valuable sentence in an assessment is sometimes: “We do not think you should pursue this.” It is also the sentence that tells you the assessment was honest.

Fourth: the assessment itself

You receive our view in plain terms — the strengths of your position, its weaknesses, the routes realistically open to you, and what each would involve. Where we recommend action, we say what we would do first and why. Where we recommend restraint, we explain that too. What you will not receive is a percentage chance of success dressed up as science, or pressure to proceed.

If you decide to go forward, the assessment becomes the strategy: it defines the first steps, the evidence still needed, and the point at which we would advise pausing to reassess. If you decide not to, the assessment is still yours — many clients use it simply to make an informed decision to let a matter rest.

What this means for you, practically

  • Keep everything. Documents you consider trivial are frequently the ones that decide a matter.
  • Write down the timeline as you remember it, with approximate dates — before memory fades further.
  • Be as candid about the weaknesses of your position as its strengths. We will find them anyway; finding them early is cheaper.
  • Be wary of anyone who offers certainty before they have seen your documents. Certainty at that stage is a sales technique, not an assessment.

If you would like your situation assessed on these terms — carefully, confidentially and without obligation — we are ready to listen.
